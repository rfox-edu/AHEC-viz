# Appointment access and attendance challenge

You are an improvement team preparing a short presentation for clinic leadership. Your packet includes a draft presentation, a shared synthetic appointment dataset, and a data dictionary. The draft contains useful evidence but needs a clearer analytical story and better slides.

All 6,000 appointment records are fictional training examples. No real patients, clinic performance, or reminder products are represented.

## Your assignment

Use your assigned story to develop a **4–6 slide presentation lasting no more than three minutes**. Your audience needs to understand the main finding, the evidence supporting it, the uncertainty that matters, and the next action you recommend.

| Assigned story | Leadership question |
|---|---|
| Story 1: Age and attendance | What should leadership understand about the apparent age pattern before deciding where to focus attendance improvement? |
| Story 2: Reminder methods | What do the reminder comparisons show, and what additional evidence would help leadership choose a reminder approach? |
| Story 3: Booking and capacity | Where should leadership investigate appointment capacity, and how should it distinguish attendance from cancellation recovery? |

## Explore before redesigning

1. Read the data dictionary. Check the unit of observation, categories, rate denominators, and the meaning of a refilled slot. Rows with identical values can represent different appointments. Keep them rather than removing apparent duplicates.
2. Reproduce the important calculations. Show both group sizes and rates where they affect interpretation.
3. Compare the overall pattern with relevant subgroup patterns. Consider whether the groups differ in ways that could affect the comparison.
4. Examine more than one visual view of the data. A useful pattern may involve composition, trends, or relationships that a single summary number hides.
5. Separate the observed finding from a possible explanation. Describe what further evidence could support a causal conclusion.

## Rebuild the presentation

- State one specific main message for your leadership audience.
- Include at least **two informative data visualizations** with readable labels, meaningful comparisons, and clear units.
- Use specific finding titles when the evidence supports them. Keep simple topic titles for setup or definitions.
- Make the essential information on the dense text slide easier to absorb.
- Repair or replace the distorted screenshot with legible evidence.
- Decide whether every slide helps your main message. Remove or reposition information that does not.
- End with a feasible next action, a limitation that affects the decision, and a measurement or study that would help.

You may reduce the draft's eight slides to 4–6 and replace its visuals. Keep the evidence accurate and use the shared CSV as your source. Teams can reach different defensible conclusions and use different effective chart designs.

## Team workflow

One person can check calculations, one can develop the story, one can edit visuals, and one can present. Combine roles as needed. Everyone should be able to explain the main finding.

Before presenting, ask a classmate to describe your takeaway after viewing the slides. If they cannot explain it, revise the message or visual sequence.

## What a strong briefing does

| Criterion | What the audience should be able to see |
|---|---|
| Accurate analysis | Correct counts, rates, and clearly defined comparison groups |
| Thoughtful interpretation | Relevant subgroup checks and careful use of causal language |
| Effective visuals | Comparisons that are easy to read and support the message |
| Clear story | A focused finding that connects to a leadership decision |
| Professional slides | Legible, selective content with repaired evidence and a purposeful sequence |
